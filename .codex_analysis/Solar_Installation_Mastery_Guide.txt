1 Introduction to Solar Energy 
 
Africa receives an average o f 5 to 7 peak sun hours per day  far more than most of 
Europe or North America. Yet millions of homes still depend on expensive diesel 
generators. Solar energy is changing that reality fast. 
1.1  How a Solar System Works 
 
Solar System Energy Flow Diagram 
Step 1: Sun 
Sunlight strikes the solar panels. The stronger the sunlight, the more 
electricity is produced. Africa averages 5 hours of full -intensity 
sunlight per day. 
Step 2: Panels 
Photovoltaic cells inside the panels convert sunlight into DC (direct 
current) electricity. A 400W panel produces up to 400 watts at full 
sunlight. 
Step 3: MPPT 
Controller 
The charge controller regulates DC power from the panels into the 
battery bank, preventing overcharge and maximising energy 
harvest. 
Step 4: Battery 
Bank 
The battery stores surplus electricity generated during daylight for 
use at night and during cloudy periods. 
Step 5: Inverter The inverter converts DC from the batteries into 220V AC electricity  
the same type used by all your appliances. 
Step 6: 
Appliances 
Lights, fans, TVs, refrigerators, computers, all run silently and at zero 
fuel cost on clean solar power. 
 
1.2 Why Solar Is Critical in Africa 
• The national grid supplies only 4 to 8 hours of power per day in most areas. 
• Generator fuel costs hundreds of thousands of naira per year solar eliminates 
this entirely.

2.2  Inverters Types, Selection, and Surge Capacity 
The inverter is the most important and most expensive single component. Always 
choose a pure sine wave hybrid inverter for home and o ffice installations. Critically, it 
must handle motor startup surge. 
 
TIP: Motor Surge Power Critical 
Refrigerators, air conditioners, water pumps, and fans draw 3 to 6 times their rated power 
for 1 to 2 seconds on startup. Your inverter must have sufficient SURGE CAPACITY to 
handle this. Always check the inverter datasheet for surge rating before purchasing.

TIP: SOC and the DoD Limit 
SOC and DoD are directly related: DoD 50% means SOC has dropped to 50%. The critical 
rule is this, the MINIMUM safe SOC for Lead-Acid batteries is 50% (do not go below). The 
minimum safe SOC for Lithium LiFePO4 batteries is 10 to 20%. The battery type with the 
deeper DoD allowance,  meaning you can use more of its capacity before r eaching the 
danger zone is LITHIUM. This is one of the key reasons lithium batteries deliver more 
usable energy per Ah of rated capacity than lead-acid.

TIP: Why Lithium Wins on DoD 
A 200Ah Lead-Acid battery gives you only 100Ah of usable capacity (50% DoD). A 200Ah

usable energy. When comparing battery prices, al ways compare cost per usable Ah  not 
cost per rated Ah.

TIP: Lithium Battery Parallel Limit 
Most lithium manufacturers recommend no more than 4 batteries in parallel. For larger 
banks requiring more capacity, use a busbar distribution system or battery communication 
cables as specified by the manufacturer. Always read the battery datasheet before 
designing a large bank. Exceeding the parallel limit causes uneven current distribution and 
premature failure.

TIP: Refrigerator Duty Cycle 
Refrigerators do NOT run continuously. The compressor cycles on and off depending on 
room temperature and thermostat setting. Effective running time is typically 30 to 50% of 
the stated period. In this book we apply a 50% duty cycle to all refrigerators and freezers 
for accuracy. 
3.2 The 6-Step Calculation Process 
1. List every appliance to be powered by the solar system. 
2. Record the wattage of each appliance from its label or manual. 
3. Estimate daily usage hours apply 50% duty cycle to fridges and freezers. 
4. Multiply Watts x Hours to get daily Wh for each appliance. 
5. Add all Wh values together this is your Raw Daily Energy Total. 
6. Multiply by 1.25 - this is your DESIGN LOAD used for all sizing.

4.1 Real-World Panel Output - The 75% Efficiency Factor 
A common mistake is calculating panel output as simply Watts x Peak Sun Hours. This 
gives the theoretical maximum. In practice, real systems lose 20 to 30% of this 
potential output due to several factors.

Temperature losses 5 to 10% Panels lose ~0.4% per  degreesC

Inverter losses 3 to 5% DC to AC conversion efficiency 
Cable resistance 1 to 3% Voltage drop in cable runs 
MPPT efficiency 1 to 3% Controller not perfectly at maximum

output

8 
Common Mistakes and How to Avoid 
Them 
 
These are the most costly mistakes made by solar installers at every experience level. 
Each is based on real failures. Understanding these now protects your reputation, your 
income, and your customers. 
 
Mistake 1 -- Skipping the Load Calculation 
Guessing system size leads to undersized or overpriced systems. Always complete a full, 
verified load calculation -- measuring unknown wattages with a watt meter -- before quoting 
any installation. This step takes 20 to 30 minutes and always pays off. 
 
Mistake 2 -- Using Theoretical Panel Output 
Calculating panel output as Watts x PSH without applying system efficiency gives results 
25 to 33% too optimistic. Always use: Real Output = Watts x PSH x 0.75. This accounts 
for temperature, dust, inverter, and cable losses. 
 
Mistake 3 -- Ignoring Refrigerator Duty Cycle 
Assuming a refrigerator runs continuously overestimates its consumption. Apply 50% duty 
cycle to all refrigerators and freezers. A 150W fridge over 12 hours = 150W x 6 effective 
hours = 900Wh, not 1,800Wh. 
 
Mistake 4 -- Undersizing the Inverter for Motor Surge 
Motors draw 3 to 6 times rated power on startup. An inverter sized only for continuous load 
shuts down every time a fridge or AC starts. Always add surge allowance for every motor 
and verify the inverter's surge rating in its datasheet. 
 
Mistake 5 -- Wrong Panel Orientation 
Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya are in the Northern Hemisphere -- panels face South. South 
Africa and Namibia are in the Southern Hemisphere -- panels face North. Getting this 
wrong reduces output by 30 to 50%. Always use a compass to verify. 
 
Mistake 6 -- Exceeding Battery Parallel Limit 
Connecting more than 4 lithium batteries in parallel without a busbar system causes 
uneven current distribution and premature failure. For large banks, use a proper busbar 
distribution system as specified by the battery manufacturer. 
 
Mistake 7 -- Mixing Old and New Batteries 

--- PAGE 35 ---
  
Old batteries have higher internal resistance. New batteries compensate by working 
harder, degrading rapidly. The entire bank performs like the oldest battery within months. 
Never mix batteries of different ages, brands, or capacities. 
 
Mistake 8 -- No Fuse Near the Battery 
A direct short circuit delivers thousands of amps in seconds -- vaporising cables and 
starting fires. Always install a correctly rated fuse within 30 centimetres of the battery 
positive terminal on every installation, without exception. 
 
Mistake 9 -- Loose MC4 Connectors 
Partially-inserted MC4 connectors create resistance that generates heat under load, 
leading to arcing and fire. Use the proper MC4 crimping and locking tool, click connectors 
fully until the lock clicks, and pull-test every connection. 
 
Mistake 10 -- No Site Survey Before Quoting 
Quoting without a site visit leads to surprises -- difficult roof access, longer cable runs, 
structural problems. These force you back to the customer for more money, destroying 
trust. Always survey the site in person before giving a final quote. 
 
 
KEY TAKEAWAYS 
• Real Output = Watts x PSH x 0.75 -- never use theoretical output 
• Apply 50% duty cycle to all refrigerators and freezers 
• Inverter must handle motor surge -- 3 to 6x rated power 
• North Hemisphere -> South panels; South Hemisphere -> North panels 
• Maximum 4 lithium batteries in parallel without a busbar 
• Fuse within 30cm of battery positive on every installation -- no exceptions

CHAPTER 
14 Panel Installation Best Practices 
 
 
The difference between a good solar installation and a great one often comes down 
to the details of the physical panel mounting. This chapter covers professional best 
practices that ensure maximum performance, long -term reli ability, and a clean, 
impressive finish. 
14.1 Roof Mounting Best Practices 
Professional Panel Mounting Guidelines 
STEP 1 
Airflow Gap 
Always leave a minimum 10 to 15 cm gap between the bottom of the 
panels and the roof surface. This allows air to circulate  under the 
panels, cooling them and improving output by 3 to 5% compared to 
panels mounted flush to the roof. 
STEP 2 
Tilt Angle 
Set panel tilt at 10 to 15 degrees for maximum annual output in Africa 
(close to the equator). Never  mount panels completely flat - a flat 
mount pools water and dirt in the frame, accelerating corrosion and 
reducing light transmission. 
STEP 3 
Orientation 
Panels must face due South in the Northern Hemisphere (Nigeria, 
Ghana, Kenya) and due North in the Southern Hemisphere (South 
Africa, Namibia). Use a compass app on your phone to verify. Even 
30 degrees off due South reduces annual output by 5 to 8%. 
STEP 4 
Roof 
Penetrations 
Every roof penetration (mounting bolt hole) must be properly sealed 
with butyl  tape, rubber gaskets, or roofing sealant. Unsealed 
penetrations cause water ingress and roof leaks - the most common 
customer complaint after installation. 
STEP 5 
Structural Load 
Distribute mounting hardware across roof rafters, not just the roof 
sheeting. Each panel weighs 18 to 22 kg. Confirm rafter positions from 
inside the roof before drilling any holes. Use M8 or M10 stainless steel 
bolts minimum. 
STEP 6 
Rail Alignment 
Use a spirit level to ensure mounting rails are perfectly level in both 
directions before tightening. Misaligned rails cause uneven panel 
loading, stress on panel frames, and a visually unprofessional result. 


--- PAGE 52 ---
  
STEP 7 
Panel Spacing 
Leave at least 10 mm between adjacent panels for thermal expansion. 
Panels heat up during the d ay - without expansion gap, thermal 
movement can crack glass or damage frames over time. 
STEP 8 
Earthing 
Earth every panel frame and every section of mounting rail using an 
earth bonding cable (minimum 6mm2). All earth points must connect 
to a single common earth rod at ground level. Test continuity from 
furthest panel to earth rod. 
14.2 DC Cabling Best Practices 
• Always use UV-stabilized, double-insulated solar DC cables rated for outdoor 
use. Standard electrical cable degrades in sunlight within 2 to 3 years and is a 
serious fire hazard. 
• Run DC cables in UV-resistant conduit or cable tray wherever exposed to 
direct sunlight. Loose cables flapping in the wind abrade their insulation 
against roof edges. 
• Never run DC cables alongside AC cables in the same conduit -
electromagnetic interference reduces system efficiency. 
• Secure all cables with UV-resistant cable ties or saddle clips every 30 
centimeters. Unsecured cables vibrate in wind, work loose, and create arcing 
hazards. 
• Label both ends of every DC cable with its polarity (+/-), circuit name, and 
voltage. Future maintenance becomes simple when cables are clearly 
labelled. 
• Keep DC cable runs as short as possible. Every extra metre of cable adds 
resistance, increases voltage drop, and reduces the energy reaching the 
inverter.

DC (Direct Current) Electricity from solar panels and batteries.

Duty Cycle Fraction of time a cycling appliance actually runs. 
Fridges: ~50%. 
kVA (Kilovolt-Ampere) Inverter and generator power rating. 
kWh (Kilowatt-Hour) 1,000 Watt-hours -- unit on electricity meters. 
MPPT (Maximum Power Point 
Tracking) Most efficient type of solar charge controller. 
PSH (Peak Sun Hours) Africa averages 5 full-intensity sun hours per day. 
PV (Photovoltaic) Technology converting sunlight directly into DC 
electricity. 
PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) Less efficient charge controller -- avoid above 200W. 
STC (Standard Test Conditions) Panel rating conditions: 1,000W/m2 at 25 degreesC. 
Surge Power Startup power drawn by motors - 3 to 6x rated
